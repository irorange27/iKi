## Design Context

### Users
iKi serves a broad audience, not only technical users. The core interaction still centers on a
local desktop chat experience where people need to move quickly between sending messages and
managing the conversation's operating context. In practice, the chat surface must support two jobs
equally well: rapid, consecutive message sending, and more deliberate control over context,
skills, tools, models, and related orchestration settings. The primary adaptation target is
desktop mouse usage, while keyboard and other input methods should remain coherent rather than
feeling like afterthoughts.

### Brand Personality
The product should feel restrained, experimental, and trustworthy. "Restrained" means avoiding
noisy visual theatrics, gratuitous ornament, or fashionable AI-product gloss. "Experimental" means
the interface can still feel distinctive and slightly unconventional when that sharpens clarity or
gives the product a point of view. "Trustworthy" means the system should communicate state,
controls, and consequences plainly, with stable affordances and low surprise.

### Aesthetic Direction
The interface should read as a focused desktop tool, not a flashy AI landing page. Visual design
should prioritize composure, precision, and legibility over spectacle. The product can carry a
quietly experimental edge in interaction details and structural decisions, but it should not drift
into promotional gradients, hype-driven ornament, or decorative "AI product page" styling.
Desktop-first affordances such as hover, focus, and dense control groupings are appropriate, but
they must remain discoverable and intelligible for broader, less technical users.

### Design Principles
1. Preserve dual-mode fluency: optimize equally for fast message dispatch and for explicit context
   management without letting either workflow dominate the layout.
2. Prefer calm surfaces with sharp affordances: controls should be easy to find, stable to target,
   and visually quiet until interaction makes them relevant.
3. Let experimentation serve usability: unusual details are welcome only when they improve
   comprehension, memorability, or flow, never as decoration for its own sake.
4. Design for trust through legibility: labels, states, feedback, and transitions should make the
   system's behavior obvious to non-technical users.
5. Stay desktop-native without becoming desktop-stale: embrace pointer-friendly density and hover
   nuance, but avoid brittle hover-only dependency where it harms discoverability or reliability.
